@section Industry Use Cases and Case Study Evaluation

@sub Banking and Financial Services

Banks run analytics whose value fades within minutes: fraud screening, risk aggregation and regulatory reporting. Work on Internet fraud detection shows that such jobs map naturally onto distributed graph and machine learning pipelines [@zhou2021internet]. Fig. {fig:finance} sketches a deployment: transactions land in replicated object storage, scoring stages run as resilient stages on a primary cluster, and a cluster in another availability zone takes over if the first fails. Our case study runs W2 on the ULB credit-card data, with its heavily imbalanced fraud label, and injects an outage during training. The outage hit before the first stage committed, so the controller moved the job to B and reran both stages, finishing in 183 s against 138 s uninterrupted. The ROC area matched the reference at 0.9868, while the precision-recall area was 0.8428 against 0.8435. That small gap shows the training stage was not fully deterministic here, most likely because speculative execution changes task scheduling, and since Section IX assumes deterministic stages, this must be fixed before stage outputs are reused across attempts.

@figure finance | Fig 26 Financial Analytics Resilience Architecture | Fraud-scoring pipeline on two clusters in different zones with replicated storage and the controller.

@sub Healthcare

Healthcare analytics combines clinical, operational and device data for treatment and service management [@batko2022use]. Two features matter for resilience: some analyses, such as capacity planning during a surge, cannot wait, and patient data may only be processed in permitted locations. The jurisdiction-aware placement of Section XII handles the second by ruling out clusters through the transfer-cost term.

@sub Telecommunications

Operators analyse network and customer data in huge volumes for planning, churn prediction and service assurance, with governance and architecture as central concerns [@kastouni2020big]. The relevant pattern is streaming telemetry, our workload W4, where a shared streaming checkpoint lets a job continue on another cluster without losing data.

@sub Manufacturing

Predictive maintenance uses IoT data and machine learning to anticipate equipment failures [@rosati2022from]. Fig. {fig:manufacturing} shows a platform in which sensor streams feed a streaming anomaly job and periodic model training. Our second case study trains W2 on the AI4I 2020 data, which records temperatures, rotational speed, torque and tool wear with a failure label. After an outage the job moved to B and finished in 131 s against 88 s; again the fault came before the first commit, so both stages reran. Here the models were identical, with a ROC area of 0.9443 and a precision-recall area of 0.6422.

@figure manufacturing | Fig 27 Smart Manufacturing Analytics Platform | Sensor ingestion, streaming anomaly detection and predictive-maintenance training on resilient multi-cluster Spark.

@sub Case Study Protocol

Each case study compares a fault-free reference run with a run in which a cluster outage forces migration and, if the feature stage has committed, restoration. We check whether the job finished, how much work was repeated, and whether the model matches the reference. It should, because the data split and learner are seeded, so any difference points to a non-deterministic stage.

@sub Smart Cities and Cybersecurity

Smart city platforms integrate transport, energy and public-service data [@fugini2021big], and spatio-temporal analytics brings its own resource management problems [@liang2023survey]. Security analytics must keep up with attack volume [@ullah2021scalability], and deep learning intrusion detection has been built on Spark [@haggag2020implementing]; losing the platform in the middle of an incident is especially costly. Table {tab:usecases} summarises the use cases.

@sub Lessons for Adoption

Three lessons follow. The biggest payoff comes from long, expensive pipelines due by a deadline, such as retraining or end-of-day risk aggregation, while cheap ad hoc queries gain little. Domains with residency rules need placement policies before automatic failover is enabled, or recovery itself could become a compliance incident. And stages must stay deterministic and idempotent, which takes care with seeds, timestamps and side effects.

Three further effects add to the benefit beyond downtime. Avoided recomputation frees capacity, because work that is reloaded rather than recomputed uses no cluster time. Operators save time, because nobody has to diagnose an outage and resubmit jobs by hand, and the decision log shortens the post-incident review. And the second cluster is not dead weight: in normal operation the placement cost spreads jobs over both clusters, so C_extra buys throughput as well as resilience. The offsetting cost, fault-free overhead, was zero for P, and avoided recomputation was negligible, so all the benefit came from downtime. Against the single-cluster baseline P saved at least 495 s, or 0.137 h, per outage, a lower bound because the baseline was cut off by the deadline; against scripted failover only about 12 s. The benefit is positive whenever N_f × 0.137 h × C_d exceeds C_extra, and SLA penalties give C_d a concrete basis [@qazi2024service].

@sub Sensitivity of the Business Case

The case depends mostly on failure frequency times downtime cost. Where cluster failures are rare and downtime cheap, the extra capacity pays off mainly if it is needed for throughput anyway; where analytics feeds time-critical decisions, one avoided outage a year can justify it. At two outages a year, 0.27 h is avoided, matching an extra cost of 1,000 or 10,000 at about 3,650 or 36,500 per hour of downtime; at twelve a year, 1.64 h with break-even costs of about 608 and 6,080; at weekly outages, 7.12 h with about 140 and 1,400. Since the baseline downtime is a lower bound these figures are conservative, and with hour-long manual recovery they fall tenfold.

@section Challenges and Limitations

@sub Cross-Cluster Synchronisation

RMC-Spark keeps clusters in step only through shared object storage and one central controller. This keeps the design simple but makes both critical: in the testbed, the single MinIO instance and the single controller container are single points of failure. Production needs storage replicated across failure domains and controller replicas with leader election, so that exactly one replica acts on each job. Fig. {fig:challenges} maps the limitations to mitigations.

@figure challenges | Fig 29 Challenges and Limitations Roadmap | Current limitations, their risks and the short-, medium- and long-term mitigations.

@sub Execution Semantics

Stages execute at least once. If a migrated attempt overlaps a late write from the old one, both write the same deterministic output, so results stay correct but work is duplicated. Stages with external side effects, such as notifications or database writes, need idempotency keys, and unseeded randomness would change results after resumption.

@sub Latency Overhead and Network Bottlenecks

Checkpoints cost time and storage bandwidth, and forcing each stage to materialise adds a pass over the data. Across sites, reading committed stages from a remote store adds wide-area latency that can dominate job time [@bergui2021survey, @mostafaei2022delay]; the transfer term in the placement cost is the hook for this, but a single host never exercises it.

@sub Operational Complexity and Cost

Running two clusters and a controller is clearly more complex than running one cluster, and operators must understand thresholds that we set by reasoning rather than by tuning, without a sensitivity analysis yet. The second cluster also costs money, although, as Section XIX notes, it adds throughput too.

@sub Dependence on Determinism and Data Size

External calls, unseeded randomness and clock dependence break determinism. Very large stage outputs make checkpoints expensive, so the adaptive policy rarely writes them and recovery ends up close to plain lineage recomputation. Very small stages, on the other hand, add materialisation overhead for little gain, and a production library would merge them.

@sub Threats to Validity

Internal validity suffers because the clusters share one host's CPU, memory and disk, so a fault on one cluster can slow the other, and absolute timings do not transfer to other machines. External validity is limited by workloads and data sizes that are small next to production analytics, and by network faults that are emulated rather than real. Published recovery criteria support construct validity [@vogel2024comprehensive], and three repetitions per cell limit conclusions, which is why we pool faults and use non-parametric tests.

@sub Limitations of the Executed Evaluation

Calibrating on cold runs wasted 8 of 24 fault runs. The single short workload never reached the checkpoint interval, so resumption went unexercised and H3 and H4 remain untested. Partitions, master kills and gray failures were implemented but not measured, A1 was not run, and warm-up made three-run overheads fragile. Each of these has a direct remedy in the full matrix, which Section XXI lists.

@section Future Research Directions

Fig. {fig:roadmap} lays out a research roadmap with four main directions. The first is predictive recovery. The health model only reacts, but any model that outputs a per-cluster anomaly level can add a signal, so failure predictors trained on cluster traces [@gao2020task, @asmawi2022cloud] and workload forecasts [@saxena2023performance] could let the controller checkpoint or migrate before a failure, as proactive cloud schedulers already do [@alahmad2021proactive].

@figure roadmap | Fig 30 Future Research Roadmap | Predictive recovery, federated management, new back-ends and agentic, explainable orchestration.

The second direction is federated cluster management across organisations and clouds. Federated learning trains models across sites without moving raw data [@imteaj2021survey], distributed ML faces similar placement problems [@verbraeken2020survey], and a federated RMC-Spark would extend Section XII's residency-aware placement across administrative domains. The third is new execution back-ends: Spark on Kubernetes and serverless analytics change how drivers and executors start [@schleiersmith2021what, @hassan2021survey], and the controller's submit and stop operations could target them without changing the decision logic; a Kubernetes back-end is the most obvious next addition to the experimental matrix.

The fourth is agentic orchestration. Language models are being tried for incident analysis and remediation [@zhang2025survey], and MLOps and AIOps aim to close the loop between operations and development [@diazdearcaya2023joint]. An advisory agent could read the decision log, suggest configuration changes or explain incidents in plain language, while the deterministic controller keeps control of every safety-critical action, as Fig. {fig:nextgen} sketches.

@figure nextgen | Fig 31 Next-Generation Resilient Analytics Architecture | Predictive signals and an advisory agent around the deterministic controller, with federated clusters and several back-ends.

Other directions concern the controller itself. Learned checkpoint policies could handle failures that are not memoryless, for example by conditioning on time of day or recent anomaly scores, as convergence-aware checkpointing does for training [@li2024convergence]. Formal verification could prove that no progressing job is ever moved. Price or energy could enter the placement cost, as multi-cloud scheduling already does [@zhu2021task, @tomarchio2020cloud]. Automatic calibration of thresholds and weights from incident data would reduce the reliance on expert judgement noted in Section XI.

@sub Completing the Evaluation

The most pressing step is to finish the evaluation this report has started. The full matrix should calibrate the fault time on warm runs, after a discarded warm-up repetition, and use at least ten repetitions per cell. It should include W2 and W3 so that resumption and H4 can be tested, and add master kills for H3, partitions for H1 and A1 for the policy's own effect. Running it on two physical hosts or cloud zones would remove the shared-host threats to validity.

@sub Towards Production

Production needs replicated, leader-elected controllers with persistent job state, replicated storage, the designed security controls switched on with their overhead measured, and thresholds calibrated on real incidents, which would also show how often cluster failures actually occur, the number that decides the business case.

@section Conclusion

We set out to narrow the gap between the fault tolerance Spark offers inside a cluster and the availability users expect when a whole cluster fails. RMC-Spark pairs resumable program stages with a cross-cluster controller. The rds library makes PySpark stages idempotent and resumable through commit markers and checkpoints them at the Young and Daly interval using an online MTBF estimate. The controller scores health from five explainable signals, detects failures with a hysteresis state machine, moves only jobs that stopped progressing, places them with a readable cost function and logs every decision with its cause. Fig. {fig:contribution} maps these contributions to the problems of Section II.

@figure contribution | Fig 32 Research Contribution Framework | Problems from Section II, the components addressing them and the evidence for each.

In 36 runs, 28 of them valid, RMC-Spark detected full outages in about 8 s and finished every valid run, while the single-cluster configurations failed every one (H1 supported, p = 0.048). Detection was 23 s faster than a 30 s timeout, but because no checkpoints were written for jobs of about 55 s, downtime stayed close to naive failover; fault-free overhead was zero against 24 per cent for fixed checkpointing. Both case studies recovered with equal or nearly equal model quality. H3, H4, stage resumption on long workloads and statistical significance still await the full matrix. The message for practitioners is that, at least on our two-cluster testbed, cross-cluster resilience for analytics needed no changes to the engine: a thin programming library and an explainable controller are enough to turn a cluster outage from a failed job into an automatic recovery, at a cost each organisation can weigh with the model of Section XIX.

Building the system taught us three things. Running the drivers outside the clusters was the most important single decision, the difference between a job that dies with its cluster and one that can be moved. Separating degradation from failure, and a failed cluster from a stopped job, avoided the main weakness of timeout-based high availability, restarting work that would have finished. And once the models were additive, explaining every decision took no extra machinery, which suggests that explainability is a design choice rather than an afterthought. Next we plan predictive signals, Kubernetes and federated back-ends, and replicated control and storage.

@sub Executed Experiment and Control Loop

Calibration (experiments/calibrate.py) wrote the 95 s fault time and 716 s deadline into the configuration; the runner then executed the 36 runs in shuffled order with health checks between them. The raw files are runs.csv, decisions.jsonl with 529 decisions, cluster_timeseries.csv and one event file per job. Every 2 s the control loop refreshes driver progress, probes masters and computes the health score of Eq. ({eq:health}), updates the state machines, applies the rules of Table {tab:rules} and exports metrics to Prometheus.
