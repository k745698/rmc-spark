@section Fault Detection and Monitoring Framework

@sub Monitoring and Observability Architecture

Observability serves two audiences: operators need dashboards and the controller needs signals. Fig. {fig:monitoring} shows how one setup serves both. Spark masters and workers publish Prometheus metrics, Prometheus scrapes them every five seconds with the controller's own metrics, and Grafana shows health scores, states, workers, utilisation, failovers and reliability scores. Such pipelines are now standard for container-based systems [@usman2022survey, @li2021enjoy]. For decisions, though, the controller probes masters and drivers directly, which keeps detection free of scrape delays.

@figure monitoring | Fig 11 Monitoring and Observability Architecture | Monitoring architecture: the controller probes masters and drivers directly; Prometheus and Grafana give operators the same signals.

@sub Health Signals

Five signals per cluster cover the failure scenarios of Fig. {fig:failures}. Unreachable fires when the master's JSON endpoint does not answer within the 1.5 s timeout or reports a status other than alive. Workers-lost is the share of expected workers not listed as alive. Latency targets gray failures: we track probe response time with an exponentially weighted mean and variance and compute the standardised deviation of Eq. ({eq:zscore}):

@equation zscore

where x_t is the probe latency at poll t and μ_{t−1} and σ_{t−1} are the EWMA mean and standard deviation before it. The anomaly level rises linearly from zero at |z| = 3 to one at |z| = 8, and samples above the lower threshold are kept out of the baseline so a long slowdown cannot become the new normal, a known trap of adaptive detectors [@choi2021deep, @darban2024deep]. Stall is the time since the job last finished a task divided by 120 s, and executor-loss the share of the job's executors reported lost. These driver-side signals show what the job actually experiences, which the master alone cannot show in a gray failure [@huang2017gray].

@sub Health Assessment

Each signal i gives an anomaly level a_{i,k} between zero and one for cluster k, combined additively:

@equation health

with weights w_i that sum to one (Table {tab:monitoring}). Severity sets them: an unreachable master (0.45) and lost workers (0.25) mean lost capacity, a stall (0.15) lost progress, odd latency (0.10) degradation, and executor loss (0.05) is largely handled by Spark. A reachable master reporting zero workers is forced to zero health. Because the model is a sum, each contribution w_i a_{i,k} can be read directly, and the two largest are logged as the cause of every state change. Such detectors are less expressive than deep models but predictable and explainable, which AIOps adoption requires [@notaro2021survey, @hrusto2025monitoring].

@sub Fault Detection Workflow

Fig. {fig:detection} shows the three-state machine. A cluster is HEALTHY at a score of 0.95 or more. Below that it is SUSPECT: still eligible, but its lower health raises its placement cost so new work goes elsewhere, and running jobs are untouched. It becomes FAILED only after three polls in a row below 0.50, about six seconds of strong evidence, and returns to HEALTHY only after five polls at 0.95 or more. With a 2 s poll interval, one slow probe therefore cannot cause a failover, and this hysteresis also stops a cluster that is flapping during repair from bouncing between states.

@figure detection | Fig 12 Fault Detection Workflow | Detection workflow and state machine: three polls below 0.50 to fail, five at or above 0.95 to recover.

Different failures land in different states. An unreachable master also counts every worker as lost, giving 0.30 and FAILED after three polls. Full latency anomaly alone gives 0.90 and a long stall alone 0.85, so both mean SUSPECT, since slowness alone does not justify moving a job. Baseline B3 instead uses one rule, unreachable for 30 s, like typical high-availability scripts.

@sub Calibration and False Alarms

False alarms move jobs for no reason; missed detections leave jobs stuck. Consecutive-poll confirmation, the SUSPECT/FAILED gap and the stall requirement suppress false alarms, while a dead or cut-off cluster always sets the unreachable signal, whose weight plus implied worker loss pushes the score below the threshold. The latency signal needs five probes to warm up before it reports anything, so a fault injected in the first ten seconds after the controller starts would be caught only by the other signals; in our experiments the controller was already running, so this never arose.

@sub Workload Diagnostics and Early Failure Detection

Because stall and executor loss are per job, the controller sees whether a cluster problem actually hurts the job, which enables the progress-aware rule of Section IX, and operators see the cause in the decision log as the score drops. Predicting failures before they happen [@gao2020task, @li2020predicting] is not yet part of the design; Section XXI discusses feeding such models into the same interface, as baseline-and-deviation detectors do for Kubernetes [@almarazrivera2023anomaly].

@section Fault-Tolerant Recovery and Resilience Layer

@sub Recovery Architecture

Recovery works at three levels, shown in Fig. {fig:recovery}(a). Spark retries tasks and rebuilds partitions from lineage. The controller restarts a crashed driver on the same healthy cluster, and moves a stalled job off a failed cluster; in both cases the new attempt reloads committed stages. Each level is cheaper than the one above it, and a level is used only when the one below it cannot help, so most failures never reach the controller at all.

@figure recovery | Fig 13 Fault Recovery Architecture | (a) Task-, attempt- and cluster-level recovery. (b) τ* from Eq. ({eq:daly}) and expected lost work from Eq. ({eq:lostwork}) against the MTBF for checkpoint costs of 2, 5 and 10 s.

@sub Adaptive Checkpointing

A checkpoint pays a known cost now to lose less later. For checkpoint cost C and mean time between failures M, Young's optimum interval is roughly √(2CM) [@young1974first]; Daly's estimate is more accurate when C is not small relative to M [@daly2004higher]:

@equation daly

for C < 2M, and τ* = M otherwise. With checkpoints every τ seconds, a failure loses on average half an interval plus the checkpoint in progress:

@equation lostwork

The rds library applies this per stage. It tracks the uncheckpointed work W, the summed compute time since the last committed stage, and the cost C as an exponentially weighted average of write times with a 3 s prior, and checkpoints when W ≥ τ*(C, M). The controller estimates the MTBF online:

@equation mtbf

where T_up is observed cluster uptime, N_f the number of failures, and M_0 = 900 s with weight n_0 = 1 a prior that keeps the estimate finite. Failures shrink M̂ and τ*, so checkpoints become more frequent; quiet periods do the reverse. Fig. {fig:recovery}(b) plots both quantities. MTBF tracking has been studied for generic systems and stream processors [@sigdel2020realizing, @jayasekara2021optimizing, @akber2021fatm]; we apply it to pipeline stages and share one estimate across clusters. The baselines are never, relying on lineage, and always, checkpointing after every stage.

@sub Lineage Recovery and Stage Restoration

A new attempt lists the job's commit markers and maps stage names to sequence numbers. Each stage then reloads its output, computes it, or in linear pipelines skips it because a later stage is committed. A reloaded stage costs one read of its Parquet output and a computed stage its normal compute time. Repeated work is therefore bounded by the uncheckpointed work at failure, at most τ* plus the running stage, the goal of RCMP [@dinu2014rcmp] and lineage checkpointing [@kweun2020lineage] pursued here across clusters.

@sub Failover Mechanisms and Workload Migration

Table {tab:rules} in the Appendix lists the rules of the three modes: none (plain Spark), naive (timeout-based active/passive) and smart (RMC-Spark). In smart mode a driver that crashes on a healthy cluster is retried there once, since a passing crash is no reason to move, and a job on a FAILED cluster moves only after 10 s without task progress. In standalone mode losing the master does not stop running executors, so a job may still finish; a timeout script would restart it and waste its progress, whereas RMC-Spark logs a keep decision.

Fig. {fig:failover} shows the outage workflow. The controller kills the driver's process group, frees its port, logs the failover with its reason, picks a target by placement cost and starts the next attempt. The log entry records the source and target clusters, how long the job had stalled, and the placement score of every candidate, so the move can be checked afterwards.

@figure failover | Fig 14 Failover and Recovery Workflow | Failover workflow for a cluster outage, from detection to stage restoration on the target.

@sub Correctness of Resumption

A resumed job must produce the same output as an uninterrupted one. Stages are deterministic, so recomputation gives the same result; outputs are used only if their marker exists, and markers are written after the data, so used outputs are complete; and stage identity combines job identifier and stage name, so outputs cannot be confused. Every output the final attempt consumes is therefore recomputed or committed from the same inputs. Section XVIII checks this by comparing model quality between resumed and uninterrupted runs.

@sub Self-Healing Strategies

A repaired cluster that stays healthy for five polls is readmitted, and jobs waiting for capacity resume as soon as one qualifies. Streaming jobs restart from their last committed micro-batch because offsets and state live in object storage [@armbrust2018structured]. Self-healing here therefore means automatic recovery of jobs and automatic readmission of clusters. Repairing the broken infrastructure itself, such as restarting containers or provisioning nodes, is left to the infrastructure layer; in the testbed the fault injector restores clusters between runs.

@sub Observed Recovery Behaviour

During the experiments the MTBF estimate gave τ* between 82 s and 112 s, while the four ETL stages took about 6 s to 24 s each, about 55 s in total. Uncommitted work never reached τ*, so P wrote no checkpoint, and after an outage the attempt on B reran everything, recomputing 54 s to 58 s per run, close to B3's 62 s. B2 did commit every stage, but it has no failover, so it could never use those checkpoints: its jobs sat stalled on the failed cluster until the deadline.

This follows from Eqs. ({eq:daly}) and ({eq:lostwork}): with a checkpoint cost of a few seconds and an MTBF near fifteen minutes, a checkpoint pays off only after about a minute and a half of work, so a shorter pipeline is cheaper to restart than to checkpoint. Stage resumption matters more for longer pipelines and higher failure rates, which is why W3 and W2 are the decisive tests in the full matrix.

@section Multi-Cluster Coordination and Orchestration Layer

@sub Coordination Model

In the model of Fig. {fig:coordination}, only the controller knows there are several clusters; clusters ignore each other and each driver knows only its current cluster. Coordination is central and asynchronous, through shared state rather than messages: the job identifier, the commit markers and the controller's view of each cluster. This needs no change to Spark and keeps the protocol simple. The price is a controller that must itself be made highly available in production, a point we return to in Section XX.

@figure coordination | Fig 15 Multi-Cluster Coordination Model | Coordination model: clusters never communicate; the controller links them through probes, attempts and shared commit markers.

@sub Service Discovery and Cluster Registry

A registry file lists each cluster's master URL, web endpoint, fault-injection container names and expected workers. Adding a cluster means adding an entry, with no code changes; in production the registry could be filled by a service-discovery system [@burns2016borg].

@sub Workload Placement

When a job starts or must move, eligible clusters are ranked by the placement cost:

@equation placement

where L_k is the share of cores in use, H_k the health score and T_k a normalised cost of moving the job's data and checkpoints to cluster k. The weights α = 0.4, β = 0.5 and γ = 0.1 put health first and idleness second. Failed clusters and the cluster being left are excluded, and ties go by name for determinism. T_k is zero in the testbed; in a geo-distributed deployment it would follow data volume and inter-site bandwidth or price [@chen2021optimizing, @oh2021network]. Fig. {fig:orchestration} shows the framework.

@figure orchestration | Fig 16 Workload Orchestration Framework | Orchestration: eligibility filtering, placement cost and decision logging for placement, retry and failover.

@sub Orchestration Policies and Workload Balancing

Placement is deliberately much simpler than Kubernetes schedulers or learned schedulers for cloud jobs [@carrion2022kubernetes, @kang2022job, @sanjalawe2025ai]. There are only a few clusters, so scoring all of them is cheap. Every decision must be explainable, and a weighted sum is easy to explain. And placement interacts with recovery, so it must behave predictably while things are failing. The load term balances work, sending new jobs to the less busy healthy cluster. A scheduler with security and reliability constraints [@zhu2021task] or a learned policy [@raj2026meta] could replace Eq. ({eq:placement}) behind the same narrow interface.

@sub Synchronisation and Consistency

Because clusters never talk to each other, consistency questions arise only through shared storage. A stopped attempt's executors may still write briefly when the next attempt starts, for example after a partition heals, but stage writes are idempotent: both write the same deterministic output in overwrite mode and then the marker, so the worst case is duplicated work. Structured Streaming guards its own checkpoint location [@armbrust2018structured]. Production fencing, such as attempt numbers in output paths, would remove even the duplication.

@sub Scaling to More Clusters

The model extends beyond two clusters with no protocol change: each extra cluster adds one probe per poll and one candidate for placement. The placement cost then balances load across a larger pool, and failover chooses among several healthy targets rather than one passive standby, a real difference from active and passive designs such as B3. At large scale the central controller would become a bottleneck or a single point of failure, so an obvious extension is to split jobs across several controller replicas, each responsible for some jobs but all observing every cluster.

@sub Coordination Protocol

For a single job the protocol has five steps. The controller submits and places attempt zero, the driver runs resilient stages while the controller polls, the rules of Table {tab:rules} are applied each poll, a stalled attempt is replaced on the chosen cluster under the same identifier, and the outcome updates the final cluster's reliability statistics. Attempts are numbered and cross-attempt state lives in object storage, so a late write cannot pass for progress.

@sub Placement Decisions in the Experiments

In every failover the orchestrator chose B with J_B = 0.000, since B was healthy and idle and A was excluded; B3 reached B through its fixed rule. The experiments therefore show that placement works with two clusters, but they never test a real choice between several candidates.

A worked example shows how the cost behaves when there is a choice. Suppose a job must leave cluster A, B is healthy (H = 1.0) and half busy (L = 0.5) while C is SUSPECT with H = 0.9 and idle. With α = 0.4, β = 0.5 and γ = 0, Eq. ({eq:placement}) gives J_B = 0.20 and J_C = 0.05, so C wins. At H_C = 0.6 the costs tie and B wins by name; below that B wins outright. A slightly degraded idle cluster beats a busy healthy one, but a clearly degraded cluster does not, and both values are logged so operators can check the choice.
