@section Results and Findings

@sub Status of the Evaluation and Hypotheses

56 automated tests check the decision logic, including a simulated outage in which the controller failed over within 16 s against 30 s for the timeout baseline; such timings verify logic, not Spark. Before running the matrix of Table {tab:config} we fixed five hypotheses:

H1 (availability): under cluster outages and network partitions, P completes significantly more jobs than B1 and B2, which cannot leave the failed cluster.

H2 (recovery time): for faults that require failover, P recovers faster than B3, because its health detector and stall rule react before B3's 30 s timeout.

H3 (no unnecessary restarts): under master-only failures, P completes jobs without migration, whereas B3 restarts them.

H4 (lost work): on the long-lineage workload W3, P and A1 recompute less work after a failure than B1, B2 and B3.

H5 (overhead): in fault-free runs, P has a lower overhead than A1 and B2, because it checkpoints only when the expected loss justifies it.

The reduced matrix ran W1 with B1, B2, B3 and P under no fault, executor kill and cluster outage, three repetitions each (36 runs). The 95 s fault time was calibrated on cold runs, and as clean runtime fell from about 299 s to 92 s, 8 of 24 fault runs ended before the fault and were excluded, leaving one to three valid runs per cell (Table {tab:results}), a low-power pilot. H3, H4 and A1 were not run.

@sub Fault Recovery Performance

Fig. {fig:recoveryperf} and Table {tab:results} summarise the results. Over both fault types the median downtime was 52 s for P, 614 s for B1, 336 s for B2 and 90 s for B3. Under cluster outage all five valid B1 and B2 runs stalled until the 716 s deadline (downtime 614 s to 621 s), while both P runs and the single valid B3 run moved to cluster B and finished (131 s and 112 s for P, 134 s for B3). Median detection latency was 7.6 s for P (7.3 s and 8.0 s) against 30.7 s for B3, and P resubmitted 8.0 s to 10.5 s after the fault, against 30.7 s. This 23 s lead, the gap between B3's timeout and three 2 s polls, barely reduced downtime because P wrote no checkpoints and, like B3, restarted from the first stage. With one valid B3 run, H2 cannot be tested statistically, though the timings agree with it.

@sub Cluster Resilience and Workload Continuity

Under cluster outage P finished 2 of 2 valid runs and B3 1 of 1, against 0 of 2 for B1 and 0 of 3 for B2. Over all valid fault runs success was 1.0 for P (5 runs) and B3 (2), 0.5 for B2 (6) and 0.33 for B1 (3). Two-sided Fisher exact tests give p = 0.048 for P against B1 and B2 combined, and p = 0.018 for both failover-capable configurations (3 of 3) against both single-cluster ones, supporting H1 for outages on very few runs. P's first outage run logged a keep decision 3 s before migrating, while the job still progressed.

H4 was not evaluated, but the ETL runs show the policy's behaviour. The MTBF estimate gave τ* of 82 s to 112 s, while the four stages took about 6 s to 24 s each, a median of about 55 s together. Uncommitted work never reached τ*, so P wrote no checkpoint and reran every stage after failover: 54 s to 58 s of recomputed work per outage run, against 62 s for B3. Resumption therefore went unexercised.

@sub Throughput Stability for Streaming

Streaming was not run; with offsets and state in object storage, a restarted query should resume from its last committed micro-batch [@armbrust2018structured], as single-cluster studies found [@dongen2021performance, @vogel2024comprehensive].

@sub Operational Availability

Fig. {fig:availability} shows mean run availability with 95 per cent bootstrap intervals: 0.57 (0.47 to 0.66) for P, 0.57 (0.44 to 0.70) for B3, 0.46 (0.23 to 0.72) for B2 and 0.29 (0.14 to 0.58) for B1. P and B3 are indistinguishable, as expected when both restart and differ by about 23 s of detection. Against B1, P's downtime was lower with a large effect (Cliff's delta −0.73) but not significant (p = 0.14) with three B1 runs.

@sub System Efficiency and Resource Utilisation

Fig. {fig:utilisation} traces a P outage run: A runs at full utilisation until the fault at 99 s, its health falls from 0.99 to about 0.3, the job is resubmitted at 110 s, and B runs at 1.0 from 120 s until completion at 223 s. @sub Summary of Findings

P survived every valid outage run while B1 and B2 failed all of theirs (H1 supported, p = 0.048). It detected outages 23 s sooner than B3, but without checkpoints in jobs of about 55 s downtimes were close (122 s against 134 s; H2 consistent, untested). P had no measurable overhead against 24 per cent for B2 (H5 supported against B2). Few valid runs and a too-short workload are the main weaknesses.

@sub Ablation Logic

We chose the configurations so that each pair isolates one mechanism: B1 against B2 shows checkpointing without failover, B1 against B3 naive failover, B3 against A1 detection, progress-aware migration and resumption, and A1 against P the adaptive policy. In the reduced matrix only the first two comparisons, plus a direct one between B3 and P, were possible. B1 against B2: checkpointing without failover did not save a single outage run, which failed in both (median outage downtime 616 s and 620 s), and it added 24 per cent to the clean runtime; over all fault runs the downtime difference was negligible (Cliff's delta −0.11). B1 against B3: naive failover turned a failed outage run into a finished one (134 s downtime), with a large effect over all faults (Cliff's delta −0.67, p = 0.40). B3 against P: P detected the outage 23 s sooner and resubmitted 20 s to 23 s sooner, but since neither resumed from committed stages in these short jobs, the outage downtime differed by only about 12 s (122 s against 134 s). The comparisons involving A1 remain for the full matrix.

@sub Comparison with Single-Cluster and High-Availability Platforms

Single-cluster deployments such as B1 and B2 lose every job when their cluster goes down, whatever the tuning, and fixed checkpointing pays its cost on every stage without being able to move a job. Standby masters coordinated by ZooKeeper protect scheduling but not executors, and do not help when a zone is lost. B3 represents scripted high availability: it survives outages but restarts jobs from scratch and, as Table {tab:rules} shows, cannot tell a dead master with working executors from a dead cluster.

@sub Comparison with Kubernetes-Based Analytics

Running Spark on Kubernetes hands the life cycle of drivers and executors to the orchestrator, which restarts failed pods and reschedules them on healthy nodes [@carrion2022kubernetes, @senjab2023survey]. That makes one cluster more resilient to node failures and simpler to run, and Vogel et al.'s benchmark was carried out in exactly this setting [@vogel2024comprehensive]. On its own, though, it gives neither failover between Kubernetes clusters nor stage-level resumption of a pipeline. RMC-Spark complements it: the controller could submit to Kubernetes, and node conditions could become health signals. Two Kubernetes clusters did not fit in the testbed's memory, so this is left for Section XXI.

@sub Comparison with Published Benchmarks

Published recovery benchmarks used other hardware, so their numbers cannot share our table; they found recovery strongly framework-dependent [@dongen2021performance, @vogel2024comprehensive], and engine comparisons show strong environment effects [@dugre2023performance]. Our in-cluster results agree with them qualitatively: after an executor was killed, vanilla Spark recovered inside the cluster through task retry and lineage recomputation, finished every run and lost only tens of seconds. What is new here is the scenario those studies did not cover, losing the whole cluster, and the comparison of recovery strategies on top of one engine.

@sub Practical Reading of the Comparison

For practitioners the comparison suggests a rule of thumb. Where cluster outages are rare and jobs are cheap to rerun, one cluster with Spark's built-in recovery (B1) suffices, because executor failures are repaired inside the cluster. Fixed checkpointing on one cluster (B2) costs about a quarter of the runtime and does nothing when the cluster itself is lost, so it is hard to defend on resilience grounds. Scripted failover (B3) already turns outages into finished jobs and comes close to RMC-Spark for short jobs. RMC-Spark adds faster detection, explained decisions and, for long pipelines, stage resumption, which matters most when jobs are long and deadlines tight.

@section Reliability and Business Continuity Assessment

@sub Business Continuity Framework

Business continuity planning asks two questions: how long may a service be down, and how much work may be lost? Fig. {fig:continuity} relates these to RMC-Spark: the recovery time objective (RTO) is the acceptable gap between failure and resumption, and the recovery point objective (RPO), measured in work, the acceptable amount of repeated computation. Cloud service-level agreements formalise such objectives and their penalties [@qazi2024service].

@figure continuity | Fig 25 Business Continuity Framework | RTO and RPO for analytics jobs, the mechanisms that bound them and the evidence used to assess them.

@sub Recovery Objectives

The outage RTO is bounded by detection (three 2 s polls plus the 1.5 s timeout), the 10 s stall threshold, and driver start-up. The first two total about 16 s; start-up took 13 s to 35 s across ETL attempts and 18.3 s and 15.0 s for P's two attempts on B. P actually resubmitted 8 s to 10.5 s after the fault because detection came in the first polls. The RPO is bounded by uncheckpointed work, kept near τ*, plus the running stage. For B3, the RTO includes the 30 s timeout and the RPO is the whole job, because it restarts from scratch; in our short ETL runs P's measured RPO was also nearly the whole job, 54 s to 58 s, because no checkpoint was written.

@sub Disaster Recovery Readiness

We judge disaster recovery readiness by four questions: is there a second place to run (not in B1 or B2), is the resume state available there (only A1 and P), is the switch automatic (B3, A1 and P), and has it been tested? The fault injection harness tests the switch again and again, which turns a disaster recovery plan from a document into a procedure that is actually practised, in the spirit of chaos engineering [@basiri2016chaos, @owotogbe2025chaos].

@sub Operational Reliability

Operational reliability concerns behaviour over time rather than in one incident. For each cluster, R summarises availability, recovery speed, job success and mean health. After all runs it was 0.74 for A and 0.98 for B, with both healthy at the end (H = 1.0). A absorbed every fault, because faults always hit the running job's cluster and jobs always started there. The score reflects that imbalance, which is exactly how it would single out a chronically unreliable cluster in production.

@sub Trustworthiness

Trust in automatic recovery rests on three things. Correctness comes from idempotent stages and the commit-marker protocol, which make a resumed job produce the same output as an uninterrupted one if stage code is deterministic. Predictability comes from fixed thresholds and hysteresis, so behaviour can be reproduced from the configuration. Accountability comes from the decision log, which records every action with its cause. Since availability depends on repair as much as on failures [@maciel2021survey], RMC-Spark improves the repair side by making it fast and automatic.
