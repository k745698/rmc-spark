@sub Status of the Evaluation and Hypotheses

The implementation and the evaluation harness are complete, and the decision logic has been verified by 56 automated tests. These tests include a simulation of a full cluster outage with a synthetic clock and synthetic probes, in which the proposed controller failed the job over to the second cluster within 16 s of the fault and resumed it there, whereas the timeout-based baseline failed over only after its 30 s timeout and the vanilla configuration ran into its deadline. These simulated timings follow from the configured thresholds rather than from measurements of Spark, so they verify the logic but are not experimental results. The measured results of the matrix in Table {tab:config} are reported in the remainder of this section. Before running the matrix, five hypotheses were fixed so that the results can confirm or refute them:

H1 (availability): under cluster outages and network partitions, P completes significantly more jobs than B1 and B2, which cannot leave the failed cluster.

H2 (recovery time): for faults that require failover, P recovers faster than B3, because its health detector and stall rule react before B3's 30 s timeout.

H3 (no unnecessary restarts): under master-only failures, P completes jobs without migration, whereas B3 restarts them.

H4 (lost work): on the long-lineage workload W3, P and A1 recompute less work after a failure than B1, B2 and B3.

H5 (overhead): in fault-free runs, P has a lower overhead than A1 and B2, because it checkpoints only when the expected loss justifies it.

@sub Ablation Logic

The five configurations are chosen so that pairwise comparisons isolate individual mechanisms. Comparing B1 with B2 isolates the effect of checkpointing when no failover is available. Comparing B1 with B3 isolates the effect of a naive failover without checkpoints. Comparing B3 with A1 isolates the combined effect of health-based detection, progress-aware migration and stage resumption, since both fail over but only A1 resumes. Comparing A1 with P isolates the adaptive checkpoint policy, because the two differ only in when they checkpoint. @sub Comparison with Single-Cluster and High-Availability Platforms

Single-cluster deployments, represented by B1 and B2, share one limitation regardless of tuning: when their cluster becomes unavailable, every job on it ends. Fixed checkpointing in B2 reduces recomputation when the cluster survives, but it cannot move a job, and its checkpoint cost is paid on every stage. Standalone Spark offers high availability for the master through standby masters coordinated by ZooKeeper; this protects the scheduling function but not the executors, and it does not help when a whole cluster or zone is lost. The naive active and passive configuration B3 represents scripted high availability: it survives outages, but it restarts jobs from scratch and, as Table {tab:rules} shows, it cannot distinguish a dead master with working executors from a dead cluster.

@sub Comparison with Kubernetes-Based Analytics

Running Spark on Kubernetes moves executor and driver lifecycle management into the orchestrator, which restarts failed pods and reschedules them on healthy nodes [@carrion2022kubernetes, @senjab2023survey]. This improves resilience to node failures inside one Kubernetes cluster and simplifies operations, and the recovery benchmark of Vogel et al. was conducted in exactly this setting [@vogel2024comprehensive]. It does not, by itself, provide failover between Kubernetes clusters, nor stage-level resumption of a data science pipeline. RMC-Spark is complementary: its controller could submit to Kubernetes masters instead of standalone masters, and its health signals could include Kubernetes node conditions. A Kubernetes-based configuration was not included in the measured matrix because running two Kubernetes clusters next to two Spark clusters exceeds the memory of the testbed; it is listed as an extension in Section XXI.

@sub Comparison with Published Benchmarks

Published recovery benchmarks were obtained on different hardware and therefore cannot be placed in the same table as these measurements. Van Dongen and Van den Poel and Vogel et al. both observed that recovery behaviour depends strongly on framework design [@dongen2021performance, @vogel2024comprehensive], and engine comparisons on HPC systems show how much absolute performance depends on the environment [@dugre2023performance]. Qualitatively, [[state whether the vanilla Structured Streaming behaviour observed here, such as recovery after executor loss within the cluster, is consistent with these studies]]. The novelty of this work lies in the scenario they did not cover, the loss of the whole cluster, and in the comparison of recovery strategies on top of the same engine.

@section Reliability and Business Continuity Assessment

@sub Business Continuity Framework

Business continuity planning asks how long a service may be unavailable and how much work may be lost. Fig. {fig:continuity} relates these questions to RMC-Spark. The recovery time objective (RTO) for an analytics job is the longest acceptable time between a failure and the resumption of the job; the recovery point objective (RPO), measured here in work rather than data, is the largest acceptable amount of computation that has to be redone. Service-level agreements in cloud computing formalise such objectives and the penalties attached to them [@qazi2024service].

@figure continuity | Fig 25 Business Continuity Framework | Business continuity framework: RTO and RPO for analytics jobs, the mechanisms of RMC-Spark that bound them, and the evidence used to assess them.

@sub Recovery Objectives

For RMC-Spark, the RTO for a cluster outage is bounded by the sum of three terms: the detection time (three polls of 2 s after the first failed probe, plus the 1.5 s probe timeout), the migration stall threshold of 10 s, and the time to start a new driver and acquire executors on the target cluster. The first two terms are configuration constants of about 16 s together; the third was measured as [[median driver start-up time from the logs]] s. The RPO is bounded by the uncheckpointed work at the time of the fault, which the adaptive policy keeps near τ*, plus the duration of the stage that was running. For B3, the RTO includes the 30 s timeout and the RPO is the entire job, because it restarts from scratch.

@sub Disaster Recovery Readiness

Disaster recovery readiness is assessed with four questions. Is there a second place to run? In B1 and B2 there is none; in the other configurations there is. Is the state needed to resume available there? Only configurations that commit stages to shared storage and fail over, A1 and P, can resume; B3 can only restart. Is the switch automatic? It is in B3, A1 and P. Has the switch been tested? The fault injection harness tests it repeatedly, which turns the disaster recovery plan from a document into a regularly exercised procedure, in the spirit of chaos engineering [@basiri2016chaos, @owotogbe2025chaos].

@sub Operational Reliability

Operational reliability concerns the behaviour of the system over time rather than in a single incident. The reliability score R summarises, per cluster, availability, recovery speed, job success and mean health. [[Report the final R of clusters A and B after the experiment matrix and their dominant contributions.]] Because faults in the experiments always hit the cluster on which the job runs, and jobs start on cluster A, A accumulates more failures than B; the score is expected to reflect this asymmetry, which also illustrates how the score would identify a chronically unreliable cluster in production.

@sub Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness of automatic recovery has three aspects in this design. Correctness is supported by idempotent stages and the commit-marker protocol, which ensure that a resumed job produces the same output as an uninterrupted one, assuming deterministic stage code. Predictability is supported by fixed thresholds and hysteresis, which make the controller's behaviour reproducible from its configuration. Accountability is supported by the decision log, which records every action with its cause. Reliability modelling of edge, fog and cloud systems has emphasised that availability depends on both failure and repair processes [@maciel2021survey]; RMC-Spark influences the repair side by shortening and automating it.
