@section Introduction

@sub Evolution of Distributed Analytics

Large-scale analytics moved from specialised parallel databases to commodity clusters when MapReduce showed that a simple functional model, combined with automatic partitioning and re-execution of failed tasks, could process very large datasets on unreliable hardware [@dean2008mapreduce]. Hadoop generalised this idea and, with YARN, separated resource management from the processing model so that several frameworks could share one cluster [@vavilapalli2013apache]. The next step was in-memory processing: resilient distributed datasets (RDDs) kept intermediate data in memory and replaced data replication with lineage, a record of the coarse-grained transformations that produced each partition, so that lost partitions could be recomputed rather than restored from copies [@zaharia2012rdd]. Spark later unified batch, interactive SQL, streaming and machine learning on top of this abstraction [@zaharia2016apache, @armbrust2015spark, @armbrust2018structured], while engines such as Flink pursued a streaming-first design with distributed snapshots [@carbone2015flink]. Fig. {fig:evolution} summarises this progression.

@figure evolution | Fig 01 Evolution of Distributed Analytics Platforms | Evolution of distributed analytics platforms from batch MapReduce to cloud-native, multi-cluster analytics. Each generation widened the scope of fault tolerance, from task re-execution to lineage, to distributed snapshots, to cluster-level orchestration.

Comparative studies continue to show that Spark outperforms disk-based MapReduce on iterative and interactive workloads, which explains its dominance in data science practice [@ahmed2020comprehensive, @tang2020survey]. At the same time, analytics has spread into the cloud: container orchestrators derived from Borg and Kubernetes schedule analytics jobs next to services [@verma2015large, @burns2016borg], serverless platforms offer analytics without cluster management [@schleiersmith2021what, @hassan2021survey], and data lakes and lakehouses decouple storage from compute [@hai2023data, @harby2024data]. Streaming analytics has matured in parallel, with modern systems offering exactly-once processing over unbounded data [@fragkoulis2023survey, @mehmood2020challenges, @almeida2023time].

@sub The Resilient Data Science Ecosystem

The result is an ecosystem in which a single analytical outcome depends on many independently failing parts: object storage, metadata services, cluster managers, executors, network links and the data science code itself. Fig. {fig:ecosystem} shows the actors involved. Data engineers build ingestion and transformation pipelines, data scientists build features and models on top of them, and platform engineers operate the clusters. Each group sees failures differently. For a platform engineer, a lost worker is routine; for a data scientist whose three-hour training pipeline restarts from the beginning, the same event is a lost afternoon. Resilience therefore has to be designed into both the platform and the programs that run on it [@isukapalli2024systematic, @rehman2022fault].

@figure ecosystem | Fig 02 Resilient Data Science Ecosystem | The resilient data science ecosystem: programs, platform services and shared storage, together with the people who build and operate them. Resilience must cover all layers, not only the compute engine.

@sub Fault Tolerance Requirements of Modern Analytics

Three trends make fault tolerance harder than it was when lineage was introduced. First, pipelines have become longer: feature engineering, model training and evaluation are chained into workflows whose lineage spans dozens of stages, and recomputing such lineage after a failure can take longer than the original run [@kweun2020lineage]. Second, failures are less often clean crashes. Gray failures, in which a component is degraded but still answers health checks, are a major source of availability loss in cloud systems [@huang2017gray]. Third, organisations increasingly run several clusters, across availability zones, clouds or business units, to obtain isolation and capacity [@tomarchio2020cloud, @awaysheh2021big]. These clusters are usually operated independently, so a job cannot benefit from healthy capacity in a neighbouring cluster when its own cluster fails.

@sub Motivation and Contributions

The motivation for this work is the gap between the fault tolerance that Spark provides inside one cluster and the availability that analytics consumers expect across clusters. Existing remedies either stay inside one cluster, for example lineage checkpointing [@kweun2020lineage] or write-ahead lineage [@wang2024efficient], or treat analytics jobs as opaque units that are restarted from scratch by a generic high-availability script. Recent work on multi-cloud Spark scheduling with micro-checkpointing shows growing interest in the problem but relies on learned schedulers whose decisions are difficult to explain to operators [@raj2026meta].

The question is not only technical. Analytics teams often measure their own success by the timeliness of reports and models, while platform teams measure availability of infrastructure, and the two measures can disagree: a platform can be ninety-nine per cent available while a single outage at the wrong moment causes a business-critical pipeline to miss its deadline [@nastic2020sloc]. Resilient data science programming, as used in this paper, means writing analytics code and operating its platform so that such outages cost minutes of automatic recovery instead of hours of manual reruns.

This paper makes the following contributions. (1) It formulates resilient data science programming as the combination of resumable program stages and a cross-cluster recovery controller. (2) It designs and implements RMC-Spark, consisting of a health monitor (C1), adaptive checkpointing based on the Young and Daly optimum interval (C2), progress-aware cross-cluster failover (C3), cost-based workload placement (C4) and an explainable reliability score (C5). (3) It provides rds, a small PySpark library that makes pipeline stages idempotent and resumable on any cluster through a commit-marker protocol. (4) It defines a reproducible evaluation methodology with nine fault types, four workloads and five compared configurations, and releases the complete testbed. (5) It analyses the design with respect to requirements, security, business continuity and industrial use cases.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section II states the problem and Section III reviews related work. Sections IV to XII present requirements, architecture and the individual layers. Sections XIII and XIV define the experimental setup and metrics, Sections XV to XVII report results and reliability assessment, Sections XVIII and XIX discuss industrial use and business impact, and Sections XX to XXII cover limitations, future work and conclusions.

@section Problem Statement

A distributed analytics job in a multi-cluster environment is exposed to failures at several levels. Fig. {fig:failures} classifies the scenarios considered in this work. At the task level, an executor process can crash because of memory exhaustion or a software defect; Spark handles this case by re-running the affected tasks [@zaharia2012rdd]. At the node level, a worker machine can fail, taking its executors and cached partitions with it. Large-scale studies show that node and disk failures are frequent enough in production clouds to justify dedicated prediction models [@li2020predicting, @lin2018predicting, @zhang2020minority, @han2022streamdfp]. At the cluster level, the master, the network or an entire availability zone can become unavailable, at which point every job of that cluster stops. Finally, gray failures, such as network delay, a slow disk or a CPU-starved node, degrade performance without producing an error [@huang2017gray].

@figure failures | Fig 03 Failure Scenarios in Distributed Analytics | Failure scenarios considered in this work, ordered by blast radius: task, node, gray and cluster-level failures, with the component that reacts first in a standard Spark deployment.

These failures lead to five concrete problems. Node failures and cluster outages interrupt jobs; with standard Spark, an outage of the cluster that hosts a job ends the job, and someone has to resubmit it. Workload imbalance appears when stragglers or overloaded clusters slow a job down, which speculative execution only partly addresses [@bawankule2022classification]. Data loss occurs when intermediate results existed only in executor memory or on local disks of failed nodes, forcing long lineage recomputation [@kweun2020lineage]. Performance degradation follows from both recomputation and gray failures, and operational downtime accumulates while operators diagnose and restart jobs by hand. Studies of large cloud data centres observe that hardware and software faults still cause frequent task and job failures despite high reliability targets, which motivates handling them proactively rather than by manual reruns [@gao2020task].

The problem can be stated more precisely with a simple model. A job J is a sequence of stages s_1 to s_n with compute times c_1 to c_n, executed on one of K clusters. Cluster k fails as a renewal process with mean time between failures M_k; when it fails during stage s_i, all work since the last durable result is lost unless it can be recovered from lineage on surviving executors. The objectives are to maximise the probability that J completes before its deadline, to minimise the expected completion time including recovery, and to keep the overhead in failure-free executions small. Every recovery action must, in addition, be justified by observable evidence that an operator can inspect afterwards.

The research problem addressed here is therefore: how can a data science pipeline running on one of several independent Spark clusters continue after a failure of its cluster, with bounded lost work, without restarting jobs unnecessarily, and with decisions that operators can understand and audit? Table {tab:gaps} relates this problem to the gaps that remain in the literature reviewed in Section III. Surveys of fault tolerance for cloud and distributed analytics identify proactive, cross-layer and explainable approaches as open directions [@isukapalli2024systematic, @shahid2021resilient, @kirti2024faulttolerance], and service-level objective research argues that availability targets must be enforced dynamically rather than assumed [@nastic2020sloc, @qazi2024service].
