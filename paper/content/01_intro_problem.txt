@section Introduction

@sub Evolution of Distributed Analytics

MapReduce moved large-scale analytics onto commodity clusters by pairing a simple functional model with automatic partitioning and re-execution of failed tasks [@dean2008mapreduce]. YARN then split resource management from processing so frameworks could share a cluster [@vavilapalli2013apache]. Resilient distributed datasets (RDDs) kept data in memory and replaced replication with lineage, the record of transformations that built each partition, so lost partitions are recomputed rather than copied [@zaharia2012rdd]. Spark built batch, SQL, streaming and machine learning on that abstraction [@zaharia2016apache, @armbrust2015spark, @armbrust2018structured], while Flink went streaming-first with distributed snapshots [@carbone2015flink]. Fig. {fig:evolution} traces this history.

@figure evolution | Fig 01 Evolution of Distributed Analytics Platforms | Evolution of distributed analytics platforms; each generation widened the scope of fault tolerance.

Benchmarks keep finding that Spark beats disk-based MapReduce on iterative and interactive work, which is why data scientists use it [@ahmed2020comprehensive, @tang2020survey]. Analytics has also moved to the cloud: orchestrators descended from Borg, and Kubernetes itself, run analytics next to services [@verma2015large, @burns2016borg], serverless platforms remove cluster management [@schleiersmith2021what, @hassan2021survey], and lakes and lakehouses separate storage from compute [@hai2023data, @harby2024data]. Stream processors now give exactly-once results over unbounded input [@fragkoulis2023survey, @mehmood2020challenges, @almeida2023time].

@sub The Resilient Data Science Ecosystem

One analytical result now depends on object storage, metadata services, cluster managers, executors, networks and the code itself, each able to fail on its own. Fig. {fig:ecosystem} shows the people involved: data engineers write ingestion and transformation pipelines, data scientists build features and models on top of them, and platform engineers keep the clusters running. The same failure looks very different to each. A platform engineer shrugs at a lost worker; a data scientist whose three-hour training run restarts has lost an afternoon. Resilience therefore has to be designed into the programs as well as the platform [@isukapalli2024systematic, @rehman2022fault].

@figure ecosystem | Fig 02 Resilient Data Science Ecosystem | Programs, platform services, shared storage and the people around them; resilience must cover every layer.

@sub Fault Tolerance Requirements of Modern Analytics

Three trends make the problem harder. Pipelines are longer, and rebuilding lineage that spans dozens of stages can take longer than the first run [@kweun2020lineage]. Gray failures, where a degraded component still passes its health check, cause much of the availability lost in clouds [@huang2017gray]. And organisations run several independently operated clusters across zones, clouds or business units [@tomarchio2020cloud, @awaysheh2021big], Because each cluster is run on its own, a failed cluster's jobs cannot use the healthy capacity sitting next door.

@sub Motivation and Contributions

Existing fixes either stay inside one cluster, like lineage checkpointing [@kweun2020lineage] and write-ahead lineage [@wang2024efficient], or restart a black-box job from scratch with a generic high-availability script. A recent multi-cloud Spark scheduler with micro-checkpointing addresses the gap but relies on a learned policy that is hard to explain [@raj2026meta]. The difficulty is partly organisational. Analytics teams judge success by whether reports and models arrive on time, platform teams by infrastructure availability, and the two can disagree: a platform can be ninety-nine per cent available and still miss a critical deadline with one badly timed outage [@nastic2020sloc]. By resilient data science programming we mean writing code and running the platform so that such an outage costs minutes of automatic recovery, not hours of reruns.

We make five contributions. (1) We frame resilient data science programming as resumable stages plus a cross-cluster controller. (2) We build RMC-Spark, with a health monitor (C1), Young and Daly adaptive checkpointing (C2), progress-aware failover (C3), cost-based placement (C4) and an explainable reliability score (C5). (3) We provide rds, a PySpark library that makes stages idempotent and resumable through commit markers. (4) We define a repeatable evaluation with nine fault types, four workloads and five configurations, and release the testbed. (5) We analyse requirements, security, continuity and industrial use. Sections II–IV cover the problem, related work and requirements, V–XII the architecture and layers, XIII–XVII setup and results, and XVIII–XXII use cases, impact, limitations and conclusions.

@section Problem Statement

A job in a multi-cluster setting can fail at several levels, which Fig. {fig:failures} lays out. At the task level an executor crashes from memory exhaustion or a bug, and Spark simply reruns its tasks [@zaharia2012rdd]. At the node level a worker dies with its executors and cached partitions. Such node and disk failures occur often enough in production clouds to justify dedicated predictors [@li2020predicting, @lin2018predicting, @zhang2020minority, @han2022streamdfp]. A master, network or zone can vanish and stop every job on the cluster, and gray failures such as network delay, a slow disk or a starved CPU slow work down without ever raising an error [@huang2017gray].

@figure failures | Fig 03 Failure Scenarios in Distributed Analytics | Failure scenarios ordered by blast radius, with the first responder in standard Spark.

These failures cause five practical problems. Under standard Spark, an interrupted job must be resubmitted by hand. Stragglers create imbalance that speculation only partly fixes [@bawankule2022classification]. Data held only in lost executors forces long recomputation [@kweun2020lineage]. Recomputation and gray failures cost performance. And downtime piles up while operators diagnose the incident and restart jobs, even though faults remain frequent in large data centres [@gao2020task].

Formally, job J is a chain of stages s_1 to s_n with compute times c_1 to c_n on one of K clusters, and cluster k fails as a renewal process with mean time between failures M_k. We want to maximise the chance that J meets its deadline, minimise expected completion time, keep fault-free overhead low, and base every recovery action on evidence an operator can inspect. The research question is therefore how a pipeline on one of several independent Spark clusters can continue after its cluster fails, with bounded lost work, no needless restarts and auditable decisions. Table {tab:gaps} maps it to open gaps: surveys name proactive, cross-layer and explainable methods as open problems [@isukapalli2024systematic, @shahid2021resilient, @kirti2024faulttolerance], and availability targets must be enforced at run time [@nastic2020sloc, @qazi2024service].
