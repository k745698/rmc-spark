@section Background and Literature Review

This section reviews the work on which RMC-Spark builds and against which it is positioned. The literature is organised by the taxonomy of Fig. {fig:taxonomy}: recovery mechanisms inside the analytics engine, checkpoint optimisation, straggler and gray-failure handling, failure prediction and AIOps, orchestration across clusters and clouds, and resilience engineering practice. Table {tab:literature} compares representative approaches.

@figure taxonomy | Fig 04 Literature Taxonomy Framework | Taxonomy of the reviewed literature on resilient distributed analytics. The six branches correspond to the subsections of Section III; RMC-Spark combines elements of every branch.

@sub Fault Tolerance Inside the Spark Engine

Spark's core recovery mechanism is lineage. Each RDD records the deterministic transformations that produced it, so a lost partition is rebuilt by re-running those transformations on the surviving inputs, and checkpointing to reliable storage is offered as an option for long lineage graphs [@zaharia2012rdd]. The same principle underlies Spark SQL and Structured Streaming, where query plans and write-ahead logs of offsets allow restart after failure [@armbrust2015spark, @armbrust2018structured]. Surveys of the Spark ecosystem describe lineage-based fault tolerance as one of the reasons for Spark's success, but also note the cost of recomputation for iterative and long-running applications [@tang2020survey].

Several studies refine this mechanism. RCMP argued, before Spark's dominance, that recomputation can replace replication of intermediate outputs as a first-order resilience strategy if task outputs are persisted selectively [@dinu2014rcmp]. Kweun et al. observed that long lineage harms both performance and reliability and proposed checkpointing the lineage graph rather than the data itself [@kweun2020lineage]. Write-ahead lineage combines lineage replay with logging so that pipelined query engines can recover lost tasks without global rollback [@wang2024efficient]. Other work targets specific Spark subsystems: partition balancing with checkpoint-based cache recovery [@li2021data], erasure-coded distributed caching with LT codes [@shang2024distributed], and fault-tolerance optimisation for iterative graph algorithms in GraphX [@he2025optimization]. A recent systematic survey of fault-tolerant solutions for distributed data analytics classifies these mechanisms and identifies cross-layer, adaptive designs as an open direction [@isukapalli2024systematic]. All of these approaches assume that the cluster survives; none of them resumes a job on a different cluster.

@sub Checkpoint Interval Optimisation

Choosing how often to checkpoint is a classical problem. Young derived a first-order approximation of the optimum checkpoint interval as the square root of twice the product of the checkpoint cost and the mean time between failures [@young1974first], and Daly extended it with higher-order terms that remain accurate when the checkpoint cost is not small relative to the MTBF [@daly2004higher]. Later work adapts these results to systems whose MTBF fluctuates: Sigdel et al. track failures during execution to estimate the MTBF in real time and skip checkpoints that would not pay off [@sigdel2020realizing]. In stream processing, Jayasekara et al. derived a utilisation-optimal checkpoint interval and validated it in a cloud setting [@jayasekara2020utilization, @jayasekara2021optimizing]; similar analyses exist for Flink applications [@zhang2021research], and FATM triggers checkpoints in line with the observed failure rate instead of periodically [@akber2021fatm]. Incremental checkpointing reduces the cost of each checkpoint [@lin2020incremental], and convergence-aware policies have been proposed for deep learning training jobs [@li2024convergence]. RMC-Spark applies the Young and Daly interval at the granularity of data science pipeline stages and couples it with an MTBF estimate maintained by the cross-cluster controller.

@sub Fault Recovery Benchmarks for Analytics Frameworks

Empirical comparisons of recovery behaviour are the closest benchmarks to this work. Van Dongen and Van den Poel tested Spark Streaming, Structured Streaming, Flink and Kafka Streams under master, driver, worker, application and task failures, and reported outage, downtime, recovery time, data loss and duplicate processing [@dongen2021performance]. Vogel et al. repeated such an analysis in a cloud-native Kubernetes environment with chaos-engineering-style fault injection and found that Flink was the most stable while Structured Streaming recovered reliably at a higher latency [@vogel2024comprehensive]. PGVal validates processing guarantees end to end and introduces reliability and failure-cost metrics for stream processors [@tahir2024how]. A survey of stream processing systems traces how fault-tolerance mechanisms evolved from replication to distributed snapshots [@fragkoulis2023survey]. These studies benchmark single clusters; this paper reuses their metric definitions, notably the throughput-based recovery criterion, and extends the scenario space to whole-cluster outages.

@sub Stragglers and Gray Failures

Stragglers have been studied since the first MapReduce deployments. LATE improved speculative execution for heterogeneous environments [@zaharia2008late], and Mantri identified causes of outliers and acted on them early [@ananthanarayanan2010mantri]. More recent approaches learn to identify stragglers, for example with reinforcement learning on heterogeneous Spark clusters [@du2020hawkeye] or neural networks over task features [@farhang2020recognizing], and survey work classifies straggler mitigation for Hadoop [@bawankule2022classification]. In streaming systems, Hone schedules outgoing tuples to minimise backlog and avoid stragglers [@li2021hone]. Gray failures are broader than stragglers: Huang et al. describe them as differential observability, where a component looks healthy to one observer and failed to another [@huang2017gray]. RMC-Spark addresses gray failures by combining master reachability with probe latency and job progress observed from the driver.

@sub Failure Prediction and AIOps

A large body of work predicts failures before they happen. Bi-LSTM models predict task and job failures from data centre traces [@gao2020task], comparative studies evaluate traditional and deep learning predictors on Google cluster traces [@asmawi2022cloud], and failure analyses of machine learning jobs in the cloud characterise when jobs fail [@bommala2023machine]. Proactive schedulers combine such predictors with remedial actions [@alahmad2021proactive], and online VM failure prediction has been integrated with tolerance mechanisms for high availability [@saxena2022ofp]. Node failure prediction has been deployed on very large platforms [@lin2018predicting, @li2020predicting], disk failure prediction has been adapted to minority disk models and to streaming settings [@zhang2020minority, @han2022streamdfp], and workload prediction models support capacity decisions [@saxena2023performance].

AIOps generalises these ideas to IT operations. Notaro et al. categorised failure management methods by their time of intervention [@notaro2021survey]; later surveys connected AIOps with MLOps [@diazdearcaya2023joint] and with large language models [@zhang2025survey]. Anomaly detection over metrics and logs is surveyed extensively [@darban2024deep, @choi2021deep, @landauer2023deep, @bhanage2021it], including a mapping study of monitoring data used for cloud anomaly detection [@hrusto2025monitoring] and anomaly detection for Kubernetes infrastructures [@almarazrivera2023anomaly]. Root cause analysis for microservices ranges from graph-based ranking [@ma2021servicerank] to explanations of cascading failures from logs [@soldani2024explaining], and surveys summarise both detection and localisation [@soldani2022anomaly, @fu2025intelligent]. A recurring finding is that operators need interpretable outputs; Li et al. stress that AIOps solutions must be trustable and interpretable to be adopted [@li2020predicting]. RMC-Spark therefore uses a transparent additive health model rather than an opaque predictor.

@sub Orchestration Across Clusters and Clouds

Cluster managers such as Borg introduced large-scale scheduling with fault-tolerant control planes [@verma2015large], and the lessons from Borg and Omega shaped Kubernetes [@burns2016borg]. Kubernetes scheduling has been surveyed from several angles [@carrion2022kubernetes, @senjab2023survey, @rejiba2022custom], cost-efficient and simulation-driven container orchestration strategies have been proposed [@zhong2020cost, @tuli2021cosco], and container orchestration has been brought to HPC systems [@zhou2021container]. For multiple clouds, systematic reviews describe orchestration frameworks [@tomarchio2020cloud], and scheduling methods account for security and reliability constraints [@zhu2021task]. Geo-distributed analytics research optimises data placement and network transfers across data centres [@pu2015low, @emara2020distributed, @chen2021optimizing, @oh2021network, @mostafaei2022delay], as summarised by a survey of bandwidth-aware frameworks [@bergui2021survey]. Raj et al. recently combined a reinforcement-learning scheduler with predictive provisioning and fault-tolerant micro-checkpointing for Spark workflows in multi-cloud systems [@raj2026meta]; RMC-Spark shares the multi-cluster goal but uses an analytical checkpoint policy and an explainable placement cost, and it is evaluated with real fault injection.

@sub Cloud Fault Tolerance and Resilience Engineering

Reliability research for clouds covers proactive fault tolerance in federations [@ray2020proactive], failure-risk-based proactive actions [@tian2020cloud], service-oriented reliability modelling [@meng2022service] and analytical availability models for edge, fog and cloud [@maciel2021survey]. Surveys catalogue fault tolerance methods for cloud and distributed computing [@shahid2021resilient, @kirti2024faulttolerance, @rehman2022fault]. Chaos engineering, introduced at Netflix as experimentation on production systems [@basiri2016chaos], has since been applied to cloud security [@torkura2020cloudstrike], containerised applications [@simonsson2021observability] and digital twins [@fogli2023chaos], and a recent multi-vocal review consolidates its practices [@owotogbe2025chaos]. Finally, configuration tuning affects both performance and stability of Spark: auto-tuning surveys and systems [@herodotou2020survey, @buchaca2020you, @li2023general, @cheng2021tuning], performance prediction [@cheng2020efficient], interference diagnosis [@shah2021diaspore] and engine comparisons [@dugre2023performance] show how sensitive analytics performance is to its environment.

@sub Data Platforms and Pipeline Reliability

Resilience also depends on how data are stored and governed. Data lake research has examined architectures and metadata management for raw and derived data [@sawadogo2020data, @hai2023data], and lakehouse designs add transactional guarantees on top of object storage [@harby2024data, @errami2023spatial]. These guarantees matter for recovery, because a resumed job must be able to tell committed results from partial ones. Studies of data pipeline quality find that many pipeline failures originate in data problems rather than infrastructure faults [@foidl2023data], and holistic quality frameworks propose continuous checks along the pipeline [@taleb2021big]. Schema evolution adds a further risk when stored intermediate results outlive the code that produced them [@chillon2024generic]. RMC-Spark adopts object storage as its durable layer and uses a lightweight commit protocol rather than a full table format, which keeps the dependency footprint small while preventing partially written stages from being read.

@sub Synthesis

Three observations emerge from the review. First, mechanisms for reducing recovery cost inside one engine are mature, and optimal checkpointing theory is well established, but the two are rarely combined with information about the actual failure rate of the platform. Second, failure prediction and anomaly detection are active fields, yet their outputs seldom drive concrete recovery actions for analytics jobs, and when they do, the decision logic is often opaque. Third, multi-cluster and multi-cloud work focuses on placement and scheduling efficiency more than on continuing a running job after its cluster disappears. These observations define the space in which RMC-Spark is positioned.

@sub Comparative Analysis and Positioning

Table {tab:literature} compares representative approaches along five properties required by Section II: recovery from whole-cluster loss, adaptive checkpointing, gray-failure awareness, explainable decisions and reproducible fault-injection evaluation. No reviewed approach covers all five. Engine-level methods are efficient but bound to one cluster; prediction and AIOps methods detect problems but rarely act on analytics jobs; multi-cloud schedulers act across clusters but explain little. RMC-Spark is designed to cover the combination, at the price of the overheads and single-host limitations discussed in Section XX.

@section Requirements Analysis for Resilient Analytics Systems

Requirements were derived from the problem statement, from the gaps in Table {tab:gaps}, and from service-level practice in cloud computing [@nastic2020sloc, @qazi2024service]. Fig. {fig:requirements} shows the requirements engineering framework: stakeholder concerns are refined into functional and non-functional requirements, each requirement is traced to a component, and each component is traced to a test or an experiment.

@figure requirements | Fig 05 Requirements Engineering Framework | Requirements engineering framework. Stakeholder concerns are refined into requirements that are traced to components (C1-C5) and verified by unit tests or by the experiments of Section XIII.

Scalability requires that adding a cluster or a workload does not change the controller's design; the cluster registry is therefore data driven and the control loop is linear in the number of clusters and jobs. Availability requires that an analytics job survives the loss of the cluster that runs it, provided another cluster is healthy. Recoverability requires bounded lost work, which translates into the checkpoint policy of Section IX. Reliability requires that recovery does not itself cause harm, for example by restarting jobs that were still progressing. Interoperability requires that existing PySpark code can adopt the approach with small changes, which motivates a decorator-based library instead of a new language. Governance requires that every automatic decision is recorded with its reason and that data movement follows organisational policy [@janssen2020data, @nadal2022operationalizing]. Operational continuity requires that the system degrades gracefully: if no healthy cluster exists, jobs wait rather than fail. Data quality concerns, frequently identified as root causes of pipeline problems [@foidl2023data, @taleb2021big], require that half-written intermediate outputs are never consumed.

@sub Scalability and Availability Requirements

Scalability is considered along three axes: the number of clusters, the number of concurrent jobs and the size of the data. The controller's work per poll grows linearly with clusters and running jobs, each poll costs one HTTP request per cluster and two per running driver, and data volume affects only the cost of checkpoints, which the adaptive policy accounts for. Availability is expressed as a job-level property: a job submitted while at least one cluster is healthy should complete, even if the cluster it started on fails. This is stronger than infrastructure availability, because it requires the job's progress to survive, not only the platform.

@sub Recoverability and Reliability Requirements

Recoverability is quantified by two bounds. The time bound requires that a cluster failure is detected and acted upon within a few polling intervals plus a small grace period for progress. The work bound requires that the work redone after a failure is limited by the checkpoint interval rather than by the total job length. Reliability requires that recovery decisions are correct in the sense of not making things worse: a job that would have finished on its own must not be restarted, a single slow probe must not cause a failover, and a recovered cluster must not oscillate between states.

@sub Interoperability, Governance and Continuity Requirements

Interoperability requires that the approach works with standard Spark releases, standard storage interfaces and standard PySpark code, so that adoption does not require a platform migration. Governance requires that automatic actions are attributable and auditable and that policies about data location can be expressed. Operational continuity requires graceful degradation: when no cluster is available, the system waits and resumes rather than failing, and when the controller itself restarts, committed work remains usable because it is stored outside the controller.
