@section Background and Literature Review

We review related work along the taxonomy of Fig. {fig:taxonomy}: recovery inside the engine, checkpoint intervals, stragglers and gray failures, failure prediction and AIOps, multi-cluster orchestration, and resilience engineering. Table {tab:literature} compares representative approaches.

@figure taxonomy | Fig 04 Literature Taxonomy Framework | Taxonomy of the reviewed literature; RMC-Spark combines elements of all six branches. | wide

@sub Fault Tolerance Inside the Spark Engine

Spark recovers mainly through lineage: a lost partition is rebuilt by rerunning the deterministic transformations that made it, and long lineage can be checkpointed to reliable storage [@zaharia2012rdd]. Spark SQL and Structured Streaming restart from query plans and write-ahead offset logs [@armbrust2015spark, @armbrust2018structured]. Surveys credit lineage for much of Spark's success, but they also note that recomputation becomes expensive for iterative and long-running applications, where lineage can stretch across many shuffles [@tang2020survey].

RCMP argued that selective persistence plus recomputation can replace replication [@dinu2014rcmp]. Kweun et al. checkpoint the lineage graph rather than the data [@kweun2020lineage], and write-ahead lineage lets pipelined engines recover tasks without global rollback [@wang2024efficient]. Other work targets partition balancing with checkpoint-based cache recovery [@li2021data], erasure-coded caching [@shang2024distributed] and GraphX iterations [@he2025optimization]. A systematic survey names adaptive, cross-layer designs as open [@isukapalli2024systematic]. Every one of these methods assumes that the cluster itself stays up. None of them can move a running job to a different cluster when it does not.

@sub Checkpoint Interval Optimisation

Young's first-order optimum interval is the square root of twice the checkpoint cost times the MTBF [@young1974first], and Daly's higher-order terms stay accurate when the cost is not small [@daly2004higher]. Later studies handle an MTBF that changes over time; Sigdel et al. count failures while a job runs, estimate the MTBF on the fly and skip checkpoints that would not pay for themselves [@sigdel2020realizing]. For streams, Jayasekara et al. derived a utilisation-optimal interval [@jayasekara2020utilization, @jayasekara2021optimizing], similar analyses exist for Flink [@zhang2021research], and FATM checkpoints according to the observed failure rate [@akber2021fatm]. Incremental checkpointing cuts per-checkpoint cost [@lin2020incremental], and convergence-aware policies target training jobs [@li2024convergence]. We apply the Young and Daly interval to whole stages of a data science pipeline, and the MTBF that feeds it is kept up to date by the cross-cluster controller from the failures it actually observes.

@sub Fault Recovery Benchmarks for Analytics Frameworks

Van Dongen and Van den Poel measured outage, downtime, recovery time, data loss and duplicates for four stream engines under several failure types [@dongen2021performance]. Vogel et al. repeated the study on Kubernetes with chaos-engineering fault injection; Flink was the most stable there, and Structured Streaming recovered reliably but with higher latency [@vogel2024comprehensive]. PGVal adds reliability and failure-cost metrics [@tahir2024how], and a survey traces stream fault tolerance from replication to snapshots [@fragkoulis2023survey]. All of these benchmarks test a single cluster. We borrow their metric definitions, in particular the throughput-based definition of recovery, and add the case in which the whole cluster is lost.

@sub Stragglers and Gray Failures

Stragglers have worried engineers since the first MapReduce clusters. LATE improved speculative execution on mixed hardware [@zaharia2008late] and Mantri acted early on outliers [@ananthanarayanan2010mantri]. Later methods learn to spot stragglers with reinforcement learning [@du2020hawkeye] or neural networks [@farhang2020recognizing], a survey classifies mitigation for Hadoop [@bawankule2022classification], and Hone schedules tuples to avoid stream stragglers [@li2021hone]. Gray failures are broader: Huang et al. call them differential observability, a component healthy to one observer and failed to another [@huang2017gray]. RMC-Spark combines master reachability, probe latency and driver-reported progress to catch them.

@sub Failure Prediction and AIOps

Much work tries to predict failures before they happen. Bi-LSTM models predict task and job failures from traces [@gao2020task], predictors have been compared on Google traces [@asmawi2022cloud], and failures of cloud ML jobs have been characterised [@bommala2023machine]. Proactive schedulers pair prediction with remediation [@alahmad2021proactive], online VM failure prediction supports high availability [@saxena2022ofp], node and disk failure prediction run at scale [@lin2018predicting, @li2020predicting, @zhang2020minority, @han2022streamdfp], and workload prediction aids capacity planning [@saxena2023performance].

AIOps surveys classify failure management by intervention time [@notaro2021survey] and link it to MLOps [@diazdearcaya2023joint] and language models [@zhang2025survey]. Anomaly detection over metrics and logs is widely surveyed [@darban2024deep, @choi2021deep, @landauer2023deep, @bhanage2021it], including monitoring data for cloud detection [@hrusto2025monitoring] and Kubernetes [@almarazrivera2023anomaly]. Root cause analysis ranges from graph ranking [@ma2021servicerank] to log-based explanations [@soldani2024explaining], with surveys of both [@soldani2022anomaly, @fu2025intelligent]. The same lesson keeps coming back across this literature: operators act on outputs they can interpret, as Li et al. found when deploying failure prediction on a very large platform [@li2020predicting]. This is why RMC-Spark uses a transparent additive health model rather than a black-box predictor.

@sub Orchestration Across Clusters and Clouds

Borg brought fault-tolerant large-scale scheduling [@verma2015large] and shaped Kubernetes [@burns2016borg], whose scheduling is widely surveyed [@carrion2022kubernetes, @senjab2023survey, @rejiba2022custom]. Cost-aware and simulation-driven orchestration has been proposed [@zhong2020cost, @tuli2021cosco], containers have reached HPC [@zhou2021container], multi-cloud frameworks are reviewed [@tomarchio2020cloud], and schedulers respect security and reliability constraints [@zhu2021task]. Geo-distributed analytics optimises data placement and transfer [@pu2015low, @emara2020distributed, @chen2021optimizing, @oh2021network, @mostafaei2022delay], as surveyed in [@bergui2021survey]. The closest work is that of Raj et al., who combine a reinforcement-learning scheduler with predictive provisioning and micro-checkpointing for multi-cloud Spark workflows [@raj2026meta]. We share their goal but use an analytical policy, an explainable placement cost and real fault injection.

@sub Cloud Fault Tolerance and Resilience Engineering

Cloud reliability work covers proactive tolerance in federations [@ray2020proactive], risk-driven actions [@tian2020cloud], service reliability models [@meng2022service] and availability models for edge, fog and cloud [@maciel2021survey], with several surveys [@shahid2021resilient, @kirti2024faulttolerance, @rehman2022fault]. Chaos engineering began at Netflix [@basiri2016chaos] and has been applied to security [@torkura2020cloudstrike], containers [@simonsson2021observability] and digital twins [@fogli2023chaos], with a recent review of practice [@owotogbe2025chaos]. Spark tuning [@herodotou2020survey, @buchaca2020you, @li2023general, @cheng2021tuning], performance prediction [@cheng2020efficient], interference diagnosis [@shah2021diaspore] and engine comparisons [@dugre2023performance] show how environment-sensitive analytics is.

@sub Data Platforms and Pipeline Reliability

Data lake research covers architecture and metadata [@sawadogo2020data, @hai2023data], and lakehouses add transactions over object storage [@harby2024data, @errami2023spatial], which matters for recovery, because a resumed job must be able to tell committed results apart from partial ones. Many pipeline failures come from data rather than infrastructure [@foidl2023data], quality frameworks add checks along the pipeline [@taleb2021big], and schema evolution threatens stored intermediates [@chillon2024generic]. We keep durable state in object storage and protect it with a light commit protocol instead of a full table format, which keeps dependencies few while ensuring that no half-written stage is ever read.

@sub Synthesis

Three points stand out. Ways to make recovery cheaper inside one engine are mature, and checkpoint theory is settled, yet the two are seldom combined with real information about how often the platform fails. Failure prediction and anomaly detection are active fields, but their output rarely triggers a concrete, explained recovery step for an analytics job. Multi-cluster research mostly optimises placement and scheduling, not the continuation of a job whose cluster has just vanished. RMC-Spark aims at the space these three points leave open.

@sub Comparative Analysis and Positioning

Table {tab:literature} rates representative approaches on the five properties Section II calls for: recovery from losing a whole cluster, adaptive checkpointing, gray-failure awareness, explainable decisions and repeatable fault-injection evaluation. None has all five. Engine-level methods are efficient but bound to one cluster, AIOps methods find problems but rarely act on analytics jobs, and multi-cloud schedulers act across clusters but explain little. RMC-Spark aims at the combination, and pays for it with the overheads and single-host limits discussed in Section XX.

The review also shaped our evaluation. Published recovery benchmarks measure one cluster, so we add the lost-cluster scenario and compare recovery strategies side by side on one engine. Adaptive checkpointing has mostly been studied for stream operators and HPC jobs, so Section XV reports how it behaves on short data science pipelines, including the case where it decides not to checkpoint at all.

@section Requirements Analysis for Resilient Analytics Systems

Requirements come from the problem statement, the gaps in Table {tab:gaps} and service-level practice [@nastic2020sloc, @qazi2024service]. Fig. {fig:requirements} traces stakeholder concerns to requirements, components and verification.

@figure requirements | Fig 05 Requirements Engineering Framework | Stakeholder concerns refined into requirements, traced to components C1-C5 and to their verification.

Scalability means that adding a cluster or a workload leaves the controller's design unchanged, so the cluster registry is plain data and the control loop grows linearly with clusters and jobs. Availability means a job survives losing its cluster if another is healthy. Recoverability bounds lost work through the checkpoint policy. Reliability means recovery must never do damage of its own, for example by restarting a job that was still making progress. Interoperability means existing PySpark code should need only small changes, which is why we wrote a decorator library rather than a new language. Governance means every automatic decision is logged with its reason and data movement follows policy [@janssen2020data, @nadal2022operationalizing]. Continuity means graceful degradation: jobs wait and resume rather than fail when no cluster is healthy, and data quality [@foidl2023data, @taleb2021big] means half-written outputs are never read.

@sub Scalability and Availability Requirements

We consider scalability along three axes: clusters, concurrent jobs and data size. Each poll costs one HTTP request per cluster and two per running driver, and data volume only changes what a checkpoint costs, which the adaptive policy already weighs. Availability is defined per job: a job submitted while at least one cluster is healthy should finish even if its own cluster fails, which is stronger than infrastructure availability because the job's progress must survive too.

@sub Recoverability and Reliability Requirements

Two bounds make recoverability measurable. A cluster failure must be detected and handled within a few polling intervals plus a short grace period for progress, and the work repeated afterwards must be bounded by the checkpoint interval rather than by the length of the job. Reliability adds three conditions: a job that would have finished alone must not be restarted, one slow probe must not cause a failover, and a recovered cluster must not oscillate between states.

@sub Interoperability, Governance and Continuity Requirements

The approach must run on standard Spark, storage interfaces and PySpark; actions must be attributable and auditable; and committed work must survive a controller restart because it lives outside the controller. Table {tab:requirements} traces each requirement to its component and check.

@sub Requirement Prioritisation

Under the MoSCoW scheme, F1 to F5 and N1 are musts, because without them a job cannot outlive its cluster. N3 and N4 are also musts for a research prototype. F6, F7 and N2 are shoulds that make behaviour efficient and auditable, and the production controls of N5 are coulds for the testbed, partly built and fully designed. The experiments verify N1 and N2 directly, and the automated tests cover the rest.
