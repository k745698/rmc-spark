@section Explainability and Reliability Assessment Layer

@sub Explainable Recovery Decisions

Automatic recovery changes where and when jobs run, so operators must be able to see afterwards why it acted. In RMC-Spark the explanation is a product of the system in its own right. Every state transition, submission, retry, failover, keep decision and outcome is appended to a JSON decision log. A transition record holds the cluster, old and new state, detector, health score and each signal's contribution w_i a_{i,k}; a failover record holds the source and target clusters, the stall duration and every candidate's placement cost. Fig. {fig:explain} shows how these records are produced and used.

@figure explain | Fig 17 Explainable Reliability Framework | Additive health and placement models yield per-factor contributions stored with every decision and aggregated into the reliability score R.

Because the health model and placement cost are weighted sums, their explanations are exact: contributions add up to the reported score, with no surrogate model. This differs from post-hoc explanations of learned detectors and from statistical root-cause ranking [@ma2021servicerank, @fu2025intelligent]. Log-based explanations of cascading microservice failures show how useful such explanations are for choosing countermeasures [@soldani2024explaining]; ours state outright which signal made the controller act.

@sub Root-Cause Analysis

For a failed run, an analyst starts at the outcome record, walks back through failover and transition records, and reads the largest contribution before the action. A whole-cluster outage shows unreachable and workers-lost at 0.45 and 0.25, while a stalled job on a reachable cluster shows stall as the main cause, and the stage events show how much work was repeated. This does not replace infrastructure diagnosis with host and network telemetry, but it answers the data science team's question: why did my job move, and what did it cost?

For each cluster the controller keeps uptime and downtime, outage durations, job successes and failures, and mean health, combined in the reliability score of Eq. ({eq:reliability}):

@sub Worked Example

In an outage of cluster A, the first poll finds the master unreachable and no workers reported, so the two signals contribute 0.45 and 0.25 and the health score is 0.30. A becomes SUSPECT and, after two more such polls, FAILED, logged as H = 0.30 (unreachable = 0.45, workers_lost = 0.25). Ten seconds later, with still no finished task, the controller logs a failover from A to B with that reason and the candidates' scores; with A excluded and B idle and healthy, J_B is zero. The next record is attempt one being submitted on B. From these four records an operator can piece the incident together without looking inside the controller.

@sub Limits of Additive Explanations

An additive model explains only what it observes. A cause outside the five signals, such as a corrupt input file that crashes every attempt, appears only as its symptom, repeated driver exits. The weights also reflect our own judgement of severity rather than anything learned from incident data. For making recovery auditable these limits are acceptable, but R should be read as a summary of observed behaviour, not as a calibrated probability of failure.

@sub Operational Transparency

Every threshold, weight and timeout sits in one configuration file and in Tables {tab:monitoring} and {tab:indicators}, so any decision can be recomputed from the logged signals. The REST interface lists clusters with state, health, top causes and reliability, lists jobs with their attempts, and returns recent decisions; a command-line client wraps it. AIOps studies find trust and interpretability are preconditions for automation [@li2020predicting, @notaro2021survey, @diazdearcaya2023joint], and exact explanations are our response.

@sub Decision Log from the Experiments

In P's first outage run the controller logged A as SUSPECT with H = 0.30 (unreachable 0.45, workers-lost 0.25) at the fault, and 7.3 s later FAILED with H = 0.24 as the stall signal rose, together with a keep decision because the driver had shown progress within 10 s. At 10.4 s it logged the failover "cluster A FAILED, no progress for 10 s; argmin J → B (J_B = 0.000)", then attempt one on B, and at 124 s the job's completion. After all runs, R was 0.74 for cluster A, which absorbed every fault, and 0.98 for B, showing how the score separates a failing cluster from a stable one.

@section Security, Governance, and Compliance Layer

@sub Security Controls

A layer that can stop, start and move jobs is privileged, so its own security matters. Fig. {fig:security} shows the architecture. The controller holds object-storage access, the right to submit to every master and, in the testbed only, Docker Engine access for fault injection. In production fault injection would be removed and the other privileges narrowed with service accounts and least-privilege policies, combining access control, encryption and integrity protection as big data security frameworks advise [@alhazmi2022big, @lu2022survey].

@figure security | Fig 18 Security and Governance Architecture | Security and governance architecture: implemented controls solid, designed but disabled controls dashed.

We built the testbed for reproducibility, not for hardening, and we say so here rather than let it pass as production-grade. Implemented are a private container network, storage credentials, an append-only decision log and per-job checkpoint namespaces. Designed but disabled are Spark shared-secret authentication, TLS for Spark, storage and API, per-job bucket policies, non-root containers and a secret manager. Table {tab:governance} lists their status.

@sub Threat Model

Three threats are specific to a cross-cluster layer. Someone who can submit jobs through the controller can use up resources on every cluster, so submission must be authenticated and rate-limited. Whoever can write to the checkpoint bucket can plant a marker beside a forged output that a later attempt would load, so prefixes must be writable only by the job's identity and markers could be signed. Someone who can trigger failovers, for instance by degrading one cluster's network, could herd jobs onto a cluster they control; placement constraints and mutual authentication between drivers and executors reduce that risk. Big data security surveys recommend encryption and access control at every layer for much the same reasons [@lu2022survey].

@sub Access Management and Auditing

In production the API would sit behind the organisation's identity provider with roles: data scientists submit and read decisions, platform engineers may also pause clusters, and chaos experiments need a separate role and approval. The decision log doubles as an audit trail, because each automatic action carries a timestamp, the job and cluster affected, and the reason. Chaos engineering for cloud security shows that misconfigurations are best found by deliberate, controlled experiments [@torkura2020cloudstrike], and with approval the same fault injector could test security assumptions too.

@sub Disaster Recovery Policies

Each job's recovery time objective is bounded by detection, the stall threshold and driver start-up (Section XVII), and its recovery point objective, in work, by τ* plus one stage. Both assume that the object store survives, so in production it must be replicated across failure domains; the single MinIO instance of the testbed is a single point of failure, as Section XX discusses.

@sub Governance Monitoring and Compliance

Governance asks who may use which data for what purpose [@janssen2020data]. Moving a job may move personal data across jurisdictions, so the placement cost can assign an infinite transfer cost to forbidden clusters, with rules drawn from data catalogues [@nadal2022operationalizing]. Evidence comes from the decision log, showing where each attempt ran, and from the markers, showing which attempt produced each output.

@sub Security Observations from the Testbed

Running the testbed taught us two practical things. The fault injector needs the Docker Engine socket, which amounts to administrator access to the host; that is acceptable in an experiment, but a production controller must never have it, and fault injection there belongs in a separate, approved chaos-engineering tool. The decision log worked well as an audit trail: 529 entries over the 36 runs were enough to reconstruct every failover and keep decision. In production the log would go to an append-only store that the component it records cannot alter, the lab credentials would be issued and rotated by a secret manager, and the object store's own access log would give a second, independent audit trail.

@section Experimental Setup

@sub Testbed Infrastructure

All experiments ran on one Windows 11 workstation with 12 logical processors and 16 GB of memory, using Docker Engine 28.3 with WSL2 capped at 12 GB (Fig. {fig:testbed}). Each cluster has a 768 MB master and two 1.75 GB workers offering two cores and 1280 MB each; the controller has 2.5 GB because it hosts the drivers, and MinIO, Prometheus and Grafana get 512 MB, 384 MB and 256 MB. All Spark containers share one image based on Apache Spark 3.5.3 with Java 17 and the Hadoop AWS 3.3.4 connector. One host removes cloud noise and makes faults exactly repeatable [@vogel2024comprehensive], but clusters share resources, so results are relative to baselines on the same machine.

@figure testbed | Fig 19 Experimental Testbed Architecture | Testbed: two Spark clusters, shared MinIO, monitoring, the controller with drivers and runner, and the Docker-based fault injector. | wide

@sub Designed and Executed Evaluation

The full design has five configurations, four workloads, eight fault settings and three repetitions, 480 runs or about two days. For this report we ran a reduced matrix that still yields every figure and table the analysis needs: four configurations, W1, three fault settings and three repetitions, 36 runs, plus two machine learning case studies.

@sub Workloads and Datasets

W1 is the ETL pipeline of Section VI, W2 a gradient-boosted tree pipeline with a checkpointed model, W3 an iterative PageRank with long lineage, and W4 a Structured Streaming job with 10 s windows. The matrix runs W1 on 1.5 million trips; the case studies run W2 on AI4I 2020 (10 000 records) and ULB credit-card fraud (284 807 transactions), mixes similar to Spark performance studies [@ahmed2020comprehensive].

@sub Fault Injection

A chaos-style script [@basiri2016chaos] acts on containers through the Docker API [@simonsson2021observability] and supports nine fault types. The matrix uses no fault, executor kill (SIGKILL on one worker's executors, repairable inside the cluster) and cluster outage (master and both workers killed). Runs start on A, the fault hits the job's cluster at a fixed time, and clusters are restored and checked healthy before the next run.

@sub Calibration of Fault Time and Deadline

Calibration set the fault time to 0.4 times and the deadline to three times the median clean runtime of two cold runs: 95 s and 716 s. Warm runs became much faster, falling from about 299 s to about 92 s, so in 8 of 24 fault runs the job finished before the fault. These runs were excluded from every analysis, leaving 28 valid runs with one to three per configuration and fault (Table {tab:results}); calibrating on warm runs avoids this and is the first change we recommend for the full matrix.

@sub Compared Configurations

B1 (vanilla) uses only Spark's fault tolerance; B2 checkpoints every stage without failover; B3 restarts a job from scratch on the other cluster after 30 s of unreachable master; and P is RMC-Spark with adaptive checkpointing and speculation. Ablation A1 was not run, and in the case studies P checkpointed every stage. Table {tab:config} gives the details.

@sub Experimental Procedure

The runner shuffles cells within each repetition so drift favours no configuration, and records every run with its decision log, time series and stage events. One script, about two and a half hours long, goes from testbed start to tables and figures, and every reported number traces back to a raw file.

@sub Metric Validity

Each metric has a known weakness. Recovery time ends at resubmission, so the start-up time of the new driver is reported separately in Section XVII. Downtime of successful runs is approximated by the fault-induced delay, which also includes unrelated slowdowns such as speculative copies competing for cores, and run availability depends on run length, so it is compared only within a workload. Because all configurations are treated identically, these approximations do not bias the comparison, but absolute values should be read with them in mind.

@sub Statistical Analysis

Runs are independent, so we use two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests, Cliff's delta with the usual labels (negligible below 0.147, small below 0.33, medium below 0.474, large above) and 95 per cent bootstrap intervals from 5000 resamples. With one to three valid runs per cell a single cell has little power, so downtime is compared across all faults of a configuration and per-fault results are only described, and completion under outage, a binary outcome, with the two-sided Fisher exact test. Availability objectives are stated as percentiles [@nastic2020sloc] and cloud availability models use MTBF and MTTR [@maciel2021survey], hence these forms.
